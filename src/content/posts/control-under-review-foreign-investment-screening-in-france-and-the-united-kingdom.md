## 1. 200 Word Summary

Recent scholarship often explains the resurgence of foreign investment screening through the language of national security and geopolitical competition. This article argues that such explanations risk overlooking the longer economic histories that shaped these regimes before contemporary security concerns emerged.

Through a comparative analysis of France and the United Kingdom, the article traces the development of foreign investment screening from the mid-twentieth century to the present. In France, foreign investment controls emerged as part of a broader tradition of state-led economic management. In the United Kingdom, a historically liberal approach to foreign capital gradually gave way to a more interventionist framework, culminating in the National Security and Investment Act 2021. Analyzed together, these histories suggest that foreign investment screening cannot be understood solely as a response to present-day geopolitical tensions. Instead, these regimes reflect deeper debates about the role of the state in the economy and the extent to which governments should retain authority over foreign ownership and investment.

## 2. France: An Alternative Economic Model

In recent years, foreign investment screening has become an increasingly prominent feature of economic regulation across liberal democracies. In both France and the United Kingdom, foreign investment screening is generally triggered when a foreign investor, or an investor with foreign ties, acquires ownership or control of a corporation beyond a specified threshold. The relevant government authority then reviews the investment, and either approves it, approves it subject to conditions, or prohibits it, typically on national security grounds. This article uses the United Kingdom as a point of comparison to trace the historical development of foreign investment screening in France, challenging the dominant narrative of how these legal regimes came to be established. Drawing on the framework developed by Hall and Soskice in *Varieties of Capitalism*, this article treats foreign investment screening as a legal institution shaped by broader models of political economy, and argues that it is a legal regime better understood as an expression of national governance and culture than as a response to geopolitical competition.

In this vein, this article utilizes Ben Clift's analysis of French corporate law, which argues that France’s legal system is a product of the historical development of French capitalism, and reflects the political and social evolution of France’s economy.[^1] This article seeks to extend and elaborate upon Clift’s argument, specifically applying his broader thesis to the history of foreign investment screening in France. To do so, this article analyzes the historical development of French foreign investment screening through the economic framework advanced by Vivian A. Schmidt and her account of France’s political-economic evolution from “dirigisme” toward “disengagement.”[^2] This article argues that, although contemporary literature often emphasizes geopolitics as the principal explanation for foreign investment screening, this emphasis risks obscuring a longer historical understanding of foreign investment screening in France as a legal tool of economic planning. Foreign investment screening can therefore be understood not solely as a reaction to geopolitical pressures, but also as an expression of France’s evolving economic ideology.

Broadly understood, foreign investment screening has existed in French law since at least 1939.[^3] During the Second World War, the French government enacted exchange controls requiring prior governmental authorization for all foreign exchange transactions.[^4] Initially, these wartime measures aimed to prevent capital flight and preserve national financial stability.[^5] Over time, however, the exchange control regime evolved into a broader instrument of economic governance.[^6] The French government used these controls to scrutinize foreign investment and restrict any transactions deemed inconsistent with French political interests or economic objectives.[^7] In this sense, foreign investment review became an expression of the French state’s broader role in directing the national economy.

Following the war, currency controls increasingly faced criticism for inhibiting business activity and limiting Paris’s development as a European financial center.[^8] Despite these concerns, stringent foreign investment screening and exchange controls remained firmly in place.[^9] Between January and September 1965, the French government rejected approximately 34.1% of foreign investment applications, denying 47 out of 138 proposed transactions.[^10]

On December 28, 1966, President Charles de Gaulle signed into law a measure rescinding the existing framework of laws, decrees, and regulations governing exchange controls.[^11] By January 1967, the French government had published its new regulations under the 1966 law, and public reaction was overwhelmingly positive.[^12] Commentators and government officials declared that “a new era of economic freedom in international relations had begun.”[^13] To many observers, the dismantling of currency controls appeared to signal France’s turn toward market liberalization and a more open investment climate.[^14]

Yet, legal scholars writing at the time noted that the appearance of liberalization did not necessarily mean a substantial reduction in governmental control over foreign investment.[^15] Even after the adoption of the new regulations in 1967, the French state retained significant oversight over foreign investments.[^16] In some circumstances, the revised framework even subjected foreign investors to procedures more rigorous than those under the earlier regime.

Through the 1990s and early 2000s, France further expanded its oversight of foreign investment, particularly in “sensitive sectors” such as national defense and public health.[^17] During this period, France also appeared before the Court of Justice of the European Communities (CJEC) in 2000 and 2002, defending its national mechanisms intended to restrict or modify foreign investments considered potentially destabilizing.[^18] In both cases, the Court ruled against France, finding that its then-current foreign investment screening mechanisms, as well as other control mechanisms such as “golden shares,” were incompatible with the EU principle of free movement of capital.[^19]

These rulings led to modifications to the French legal framework.[^20] In response, France introduced reforms in 2005 through the Monetary and Financial Code to align domestic law with EU requirements while preserving national discretion over foreign investment screening.[^21] The revised framework retained the ability to screen foreign investment but defined the categories of transactions subject to authorization more precisely.[^22] The Minister of Economy could refuse approval where a foreign investment posed risks to national security, public health, or other public order interests.[^23]

Further adjustments followed. Decrees in 2014 and 2018 expanded the list of “sensitive” sectors under the Code.[^24] Between 2019 and 2021, France undertook a broader modernization of its foreign investment screening regime, strengthening procedural controls and updating the thresholds for review.[^25] These reforms form the basis of the current framework governing foreign investment control in France.[^26]

If Clift is correct that French corporate law on a broader scale is representative of the evolution of French capitalism, it follows that the individual components of that legal system can be an expression of this trend. This article argues that the development of foreign investment screening is compatible with Schmidt's historical account of French economic progress.

Schmidt argues that while France’s dominant economic ideology has rarely been linear across the Republics, there has been a clear trend towards statism that persisted even during periods of market liberalism, such as the Third Republic.[^27] She points toward ‘Dirigisme,’ an approach to economic policy that Schmidt attributes to Louis XIV and his minister Colbert, which can most simply be defined as a pattern of policy-making in which governments formulate directing, wide-reaching economic policies absent of business input.[^28] It is a conception of capitalism in which the government leads, and corporations follow, compared to market liberalism, where the government retroactively regulates behavior in certain instances of corporate leadership.[^29] In recent history, Schmidt argues that by joining the European Economic Community, France’s ability to maintain its own economic ideology separate from market liberalism was severely hampered, and subsequently, the state’s ability to intervene in the economy has been procedurally stunted despite various governments expressing strong desires to do so.[^30] To both Schmidt and Clift, France’s compliance with the EU’s liberal market values in law and regulation has produced a form of ‘Frankenstein’ capitalism, in which the values of the French state and those who wish to wield it are in tension with one another.[^31] As a result, France tends towards what is described as protectionist capitalism, which Schmidt interprets as the state’s attempt to steer France back to the economic policy it has known, dirigisme, while also being constrained by EU compliance.[^32]

The strictness of foreign investment screening in France was only substantially modified and relaxed following the two cases before the CJEC, in which France was found to be in violation of the 1957 Treaty of Rome.[^33] While the reforms in 1966 marked a period of outward-looking liberalization, perhaps also motivated by developments within the European Community, the state retained significant control and oversight over foreign investment.[^34] Through the legal history of foreign investment screening, a picture of the economic relations between France and the EU as described by Schmidt emerges. In 2000 and 2002, France was forced to defend its preferred economic policy before an EU institution and lost.[^35] This resulted in France converting its economic legal regime towards one more reflective of a liberal market society, such as the U.S.A. and the UK, meaning investments can only be screened when there is a national security risk. This is a push to treat foreign investment screening not as a control on monetary flows and broader economic planning, but rather as a hands-off regulation imposed only in specific instances. In response, France modified its legal language, adopting the liberal tone of ‘national security’ while in 2014, 2018, and in 2019 until 2023, still pushing to have greater state control over monetary flows.[^36] This is quite reflective of the trend both Clift and Schmidt depict: France attempting to integrate a form of dirigisme into an EU-compliant regulatory framework.[^37]

In recent years, foreign investment screening has grown stricter across liberal market economies due to national security concerns, primarily directed at China.[^38] In much of the literature on France, the recent increase in restrictions has been seen as a reflection of this trend.[^39] However, the history outlined in this article is at odds with this narrative. While this paper does not deny the role geopolitics plays in any foreign investment screening regime, it is perhaps a correlated rather than a causal event. Given France's legal record of pushing toward a stricter foreign investment screening regime since 1939, and its economic ideology aligned with such measures since the reign of Louis XIV, it is equally plausible that a recent strengthening of foreign investment screening is an expression of France’s relationship with the EU. If France has always wanted to engage in this activity, and it is only recently that the EU has given its members the go-ahead on these regimes for security reasons, around 2019, it follows that France has perhaps co-opted liberal market language to justify a legal regime it has wanted to implement on broader economic and ideological grounds.[^40] This is to say, perhaps security is an excuse, not the reason.

This broader economic argument is also reflected in the CJEC’s 2000 decision. “...\[W\]hile Member States are still, in principle, free to determine the requirements of public policy and public security in the light of their national needs, those grounds must ... as derogations from the fundamental principle of free movement of capital, be interpreted strictly, so that their scope cannot be determined unilaterally by each Member State without any control by the Community institutions. Thus, public policy and public security may be relied on only if there is a genuine and sufficiently serious threat to a fundamental interest of society. Moreover, those derogations must not be misapplied so as, in fact, to serve purely economic ends.”[^41] The court’s language positions a stance of market liberalism, holding that the movement of capital is a right that precedes government regulation. As a legal ethicist would argue, this is not a ‘neutral’ legal stance but an enforcement of a political ideal. One in which France’s legal history produces a tension with. Not all rights are absolute, and in many ways, the EU restricts the free movement of capital through other regulatory schemes. What is viewed as a legitimate restriction of a right is largely cultural, and it is thus fascinating to illustrate how France diverges from its European partners in this specific legal and economic framework.

## 3. The United Kingdom: A Liberal Comparison

This cultural divergence is exemplified by the United Kingdom (UK), whose economic history of foreign investment screening regimes contrasts with French economic justifications and role within the European Community. The UK exemplifies what Hall and Soskice term a liberal market economy, in which economic actors coordinate primarily through competitive markets and arm’s-length contracting, whereas France is treated as an ambiguous case that resists clear classification. This distinction helps explain not only why early UK foreign investment policy was framed in competition terms rather than industrial strategy, but why, even as security concerns mounted in the 2010s, the resulting instrument, the National Security and Investment Act (NSIA) 2021, retained the liberal market’s characteristic posture. While the NSIA is broad in reach, it remained tethered to a primary statutory justification, national security, rather than the broad discretionary authority for economic direction retained in French law.

This focus on security, however, is a relatively recent development in UK policy. The UK’s early approach to Foreign Direct Investment resembles a series of laws and policies that, taken together, point to a framework primarily oriented toward competition concerns rather than national security. The Exchange Control Act 1947 was enacted in the period immediately following the Second World War. Administered by the Bank of England on behalf of the Treasury, it established broad government authority over foreign exchange transactions to protect a struggling post-war British economy. It prevented British citizens and corporations from holding foreign currencies, from conducting financial transfers abroad without approval, and from providing sterling-denominated credit to non-residents, to prevent the devaluation of the Pound.[^42] While it appears comparable to the French exchange controls of that same era, its underlying purpose appears distinct. While France’s wartime exchange controls originated with a similar purpose, preventing capital flight and preserving financial stability, that regime would, over the subsequent decades, evolve into an instrument of more investor-specific scrutiny in a way the British framework did not. Ultimately, the Act was repealed entirely by the Thatcher government in 1979.[^43] Although scholars contest the original motivations behind the 1979 abolition of the Exchange Control Act, capital account liberalization became an entrenched policy framework thereafter.[^44] Davis and Walsh’s analysis of UK budget statements and policy from 1976 to 2010 shows a sustained 'free-market economic consensus'[^45] with the UK’s commitment to openness comparatively more thorough than their peer economies in the EU, which maintained more robust domestic industry protections.[^46]

In 1965, the Monopolies and Mergers Act introduced a formal mechanism for reviewing mergers on public interest grounds, extending the scope of the Monopolies Commission established under the Monopolies and Restrictive Practices Act 1948.[^47] The 1965 Act permitted the Board of Trade to refer proposed mergers to the Commission when they met certain asset size thresholds.[^48] The Fair Trading Act 1973 built on this framework by centralizing investigation and review through the Office of Fair Trading and by consolidating the public-interest standard for merger review. The breadth of this test complicates a straightforward liberal market economy characterization. The Commission evaluated transactions based on a multi-factor test that included considerations of competitiveness, employment, efficiency, industry balance, and innovation.[^49] This awarded the Commission discretion broad enough to evaluate the objectives, such as employment and industry balance, that France’s regime pursued more directly. However, this standard has faced scholarly criticism for rendering the mergers review system overly vague and opaque, as the breadth of the 'public interest' test gave limited predictability as to how decisions would be reached.[^50] Partly in response to this unpredictability, economic policy shifts towards a more liberal approach eventually gave rise to the Tebbit doctrine, which prioritized assessment purely through the lens of competition and laid the conceptual groundwork for the Enterprise Act 2002.[^51]

Nevertheless, various episodes throughout the 2010s revealed a tension between the liberal framework and concerns about foreign ownership of strategically sensitive industries. For instance, the 2009-10 hostile takeover of Cadbury Kraft Foods generated sufficient public and parliamentary concern that the House of Commons Business, Innovation and Skills Committee opened a formal inquiry, concluding that the deal was 'likely to shape public policy' on takeovers and contributing to the Takeover Panel’s 2011 revision of the Takeover Code.[^52] That the UK’s initial response operated through the Takeover Code, rather than a standalone ownership-screening mechanism, is itself consistent with a liberal market economy’s characteristic reliance on market-based, procedural safeguards over direct ownership review. Such unease was especially pronounced in the UK defence industrial sector, where academics and policy-makers increasingly noted the vulnerability of UK defence contractors to foreign acquisition and the absence of an adequate mechanism to address that gap.[^53] The 2020 attempted takeover of Imagination Technologies, a UK semiconductor design company, by Chinese-backed Canyon Bridge, lent urgency to the National Security and Investment Bill, first announced in December 2020 and building on the 2018 White Paper’s proposed expansion of the state’s intervention powers.[^54] The scale of the shortfall of previous legislation was noted by peers debating the NSI Bill in 2021, positing that only 12 acquisitions had been reviewed by the EA 2002, even as the sophistication of national security threats had materially increased. [^55]

Concurrently, the government increasingly formalized concerns about Chinese investments in sensitive sectors and aligned with the United States’ foreign investment screening framework. [^56] The NSIA’s application reflects these concerns in practice, as eight out of the fifteen final orders issued under the Act in the 2022-23 period involved an acquirer linked to China, a figure particularly notable given the Act’s prima facie neutral terms and lack of reference to a specific country. When creating the NSIA, the UK government openly used FIRRMA as a starting point for its legislation, and many legal scholars have noted the structural similarities, including the adoption of a 'national security' trigger for review, its delineation of sensitive factors, its cross-border application in specific circumstances, and the combination of both mandatory and voluntary notification requirements. FIRRMA’s drafting history also reveals substantial concerns over Chinese investments in critical sectors as a driver. This convergence is unsurprising given that both states operate within a similar liberal institutional framework.

The NSIA represented the culmination of the process outlined in the preceding sections.[^57] As the first standalone statutory framework for screening foreign investment on national security grounds, the Act empowers the Secretary of State to “call in” any acquisition of control over a qualifying entity or asset, irrespective of the acquirer’s nationality, for a national security assessment. It introduces a mandatory notification requirement for acquisitions in certain types of oil, gas and electricity production, transportation, and supply infrastructure, as well as certain nuclear activities, among 17 prescribed sectors of the economy where shaleholding or voting thresholds are met. The Secretary of State’s powers extend to acquisitions completed up to five years before the Act’s inception, and a notifiable acquisition completed without the Secretary of State’s approval is automatically void.

As such, the NSIA bears a resemblance to France’s current FDI screening framework, both grant the executive broad discretion over acquisitions in sensitive sectors and deploy national security as the primary trigger. This convergence may be explained in part by CJEC rulings in 2000 and 2002, under which France was required to narrow its grounds for screening to a national security framework more consistent with the principles of free movement in the EU. Yet where the NSIA confines review to national security concerns, France’s framework also authorizes review on public health and order grounds, categories that remain broad enough for objectives beyond security, and one consistent with France’s pattern of expanding the scope of its regime. Extending the logic of Hall and Soskice’s argument, formal convergence in legal instruments, such as foreign investment screening, does not necessarily indicate a convergence in purpose. This logic, applied here, suggests the same legal mechanism may serve different underlying objectives in different national contexts. In this case, France adopted a national security language due to constraints imposed by EU compliance standards, within a tradition of state-directed economic management that it has continued to extend where permitted. In contrast, the UK adopted a comparable system as a targeted corrective in an otherwise market-oriented framework it had maintained for decades. The same national security justification, in essence, expresses different institutional histories.
